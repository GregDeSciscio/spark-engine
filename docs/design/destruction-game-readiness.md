# Destruction: what a first game still needs

Written 2026-10-30, at `a7b99f9`, before any game uses the `Destruction` subsystem (ADR-011). The bar is a shipped game, not the sandbox: a level of several structures, a player, bots, checkpoints and the rest of a frame's work on top. Updated the same day, after the vertical slice (`apps/slice`) was built and the gaps below were worked through; each status says what closed it and how it is held.

## Where it stood

The subsystem was sound on its own terms:
- **Strain** breaks exactly what an always-refactor solver breaks, step for step, in all 16 bench cascades. A step with 1–2 breaks costs 2–10 ms up to 6,346 chunks.
- **The solve** runs in an engine-owned worker that falls back to the main thread, replaying every request so far, if the worker fails.
- **A game wires it in three lines.** A raycast names the structure's entity and the chunk, and `dispose()` leaves no body, collider or joint behind.
- **Determinism:** randomness is forked from the context's `Random` per structure and per layer, and nothing in the simulation reads the clock.
- **Checks:** typecheck, lint and 532 tests pass. `apps/destruction` has the soak, feel, rest, clip, tower and mid-air probes that caught every regression so far.

What it had not had is a game. Every measurement was one structure in the sandbox with a fly camera. No player, bot or authored level had been in it.

## Where it stands now

`apps/slice` is that game-shaped scene: a yard authored in Blender (`pnpm level:yard`) with three destructible buildings, a player on the shoulder camera, five bots on a navmesh that follows the damage, damage from debris and gas, and checkpoints. Its probe (`pnpm --filter @spark/slice probe`, 13 checks) and two `pnpm perf` rows hold what it proved. Building it found two engine bugs no sandbox run could: a collider leaked when game code destroyed a body from inside a physics query, and a landed top bounced back almost to where it was built. Both are fixed and held by tests. Typecheck, lint, 553 tests and every app build pass.

## The gaps

| # | Gap | Against | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Buildings are procedural only.** A structure is `Piece`s from `core/building.ts`: walls and slabs cut into 2D Voronoi cells (`fractureRect`) and extruded (`prism`). Nothing fractures a Blender mesh or reads connections from glTF extras, so a level's buildings cannot be authored the way the rest of the level is. | ADR-008 | **closed**: `spark.type = destructible` on a box in a level (`spark.structure`, `spark.piece` wall/slab/column, optional `spark.cell` and `spark.density`). The loader hides the mesh, gives it no collider and returns the boxes in `LoadedLevel.destructibles`. `authoredStructure` fractures each box through its thinnest axis, turned any way, glued to the lowest point as ground. The yard's three buildings are authored this way, one of them turned 20°. Tests: `DestructionAuthored.test.ts`. Not done: arbitrary meshes (a box per wall panel, slab or column is the authoring unit). |
| 2 | **Never more than one structure in a scene.** Each `Destruction` made its own `WorkerStrainHost` and its own `Debris` with `maxBodies: 360`, so ten buildings meant ten workers and up to 3,600 loose bodies. | a level | **closed**: the structures of one physics world share one body budget and one link budget (`budgetsOf`: the largest limit any asks for, each shedding its share of the excess) and one strain worker (`StrainWorkerPool`: each structure's solver by id, made with the first, gone with the last, every structure falling back together). In the slice, three buildings coming down at once peaked at 258–283 bodies against 360. Tests: `DestructionShared.test.ts`. |
| 3 | **The navmesh does not change.** `ai/Navigation.ts` baked once, and destruction emitted nothing navigation read. Bots would walk into rubble, never use a new hole, and route across floors that had gone. | ADR-005 (bots) | **closed**: `Navigation.tiled(geometry, bounds)` rebuilds tiles where `invalidate` says, from a callback that returns the world as it is now; `Destruction.navGeometry` gives standing chunks and rubble, and a `reshape` event says where they changed. In the slice, the depot's back wall taken out shortens a way in from 28.8 m to 5.5 m once 2 tiles are rebuilt. Tests: `DestructionNavigation.test.ts`. Cost: 5–7 ms a 6.4 m tile beside a five-storey building, nearly all of it Recast; the slice rebuilds one a frame. |
| 4 | **No character has stood in it.** No test put one on a floor that drops or under falling debris, no event named an entity that was hit, and gas did no damage. | a player | **closed**: `Destruction.watchHits(physics, eid)` reports debris striking a watched entity as a `hit` (the chunk, mass, the speed it came in at, momentum), once a body. `Destruction.densityAt` reads smoke or gas at a point over every structure, and `Destruction.nearest` gives a grenade its structure. With the engine's character controller, a character on a floor that loses its supports rides it down. One under it is hit by the storeys above and, being kinematic, holds them up until the game takes it away, when they come the rest of the way. In the slice a heavy hit kills, and so does gas. Tests: `DestructionCharacters.test.ts`. |
| 5 | **The floating fix has not been playtested.** Greg reported floating tops four times; `a7b99f9` fixed the root cause and every probe passed, but nobody had played it since. Its trade-off is a gameplay question: a building that comes all the way down leaves mostly slabs and grit (26% of a five-storey building gone, 60% of an eight-storey one), which decides what cover and sightlines remain. | playtest | **open, needs Greg.** The slice timed every top it brought down and found a new float: a top that landed on the storey under it bounced back up, 2.9 m on a two-storey building, nearly where it was built, for half a second. Debris now leaves an impact upward at no more than a tenth of its fall speed, and the slice's tops go straight down (`a top that lands on the storey under it does not bounce back up` in `Destruction.test.ts`; the probe's two fall checks). What is left is play: the slice is the place to judge both the falls and what a collapse leaves. |
| 6 | **A camera path changed a fixed-clock simulation.** While making the showcase film, a fixed-clock replay with a different camera path gave a different simulation. | ADR-005 (determinism kept regardless) | **closed: not a bug.** `pnpm --filter @spark/destruction determinism` runs the sandbox three times on a fixed clock with the same events at world points, twice on one camera path and once on another. Every rigid body's pose, hashed to the bit, matches at every checkpoint. The film's difference came from its inputs: shots aimed through the camera, or a different per-frame routine. A vitest case holds the engine side. |
| 7 | **Performance is measured alone.** Every number came from one structure on a Ryzen 7 3700X and an idle RTX 4070, with nothing else in the frame. Destruction needed its own budget in `pnpm perf`, and a run on a weaker machine. | perf suite | **closed for this machine; open for slower ones.** `pnpm perf` has `slice-yard` (116–123 fps, 6.6 ms CPU) and `slice-collapse` (88–89 fps, 10.7 ms CPU, three buildings coming down in its window). In real time the slice's collapse ran at a 12 ms median frame, 24–36 ms longest, 0–1 frames over 33 ms in three runs, with the bots and the navmesh going. On a slower CPU (Chromium's throttling, which slows the CPU and not the GPU) it does not hold. Throttled 2×, the yard stands at 12 ms but the collapse runs at a 42 ms median (about 24 fps) for several seconds, and 36 navmesh tiles are still waiting at the end. Levers, untried: a smaller body budget on lower quality presets, navmesh tiles rebuilt in a worker or by a millisecond budget, and fewer physics catch-up steps under load. Nothing has been measured on a real slower machine. |
| 8 | **No save or restore of destroyed state.** A checkpoint could only rebuild the structure, or replay what broke it. | mission shape (checkpoints) | **closed**: `save()` gives a structure's destroyed state as plain data: gone chunks, rubble by settling, loose bodies with their velocities, broken glue, hits and soot (about 5 KB for a crater and a corner down). `restore()` puts the same structure, built afresh, back into it. Not kept: hinges and nets, smoke, gravel, dents and resting loads. In the slice, a checkpoint brings back all three buildings, the dead bots and the player. Tests: `DestructionSave.test.ts`. |
| 9 | **Smaller items.** The sound takes passed the signature and mix checks but were never signed off by ear. Broken faces are flat (their relief is shading) and soot is one shade per chunk. ADR-011 still read "Proposed". ADR-004 rules out a first-person camera, so a THE FINALS-style first-person game would reopen it. | sandbox README, ADRs | **partly closed**: ADR-011 is accepted and describes the shared worker and budgets. Still open: the sounds need a listen (Greg), broken faces and soot are as they were, and ADR-004 stands until a first-person game is chosen. |

## What to do next

1. **Play the slice** (gap 5): `pnpm dev:slice`. Bring the buildings down from the ground storey, the side and the top, and judge the falls and the heaps. The probe's fall timings say where to look if a top seems to hang.
2. **Decide the first game.** The Task Unit sequel (ADR-005) is third-person, authored in Blender and has bots, and everything it needs from destruction is now in place. A first-person game would reopen ADR-004 first.
3. **Before shipping on slower machines** (gap 7): budget the debris and the navmesh tiles by quality preset, then measure on a real mid-range laptop.
