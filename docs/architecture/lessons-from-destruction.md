# Lessons from destruction

What building the THE FINALS-style destruction sandbox taught, first in its own repo and then moving into the engine as `packages/engine/src/destruction` (ADR-011). The sandbox is `apps/destruction`; its README has the measurements these came from. The engine changes it forced (the physics handle layer, sharded fixed bodies, the engine's own joint index) are in `lessons-from-the-showcase.md`'s table and ADR-010.

## From the sandbox

- **Gradual destruction needs assembly-level loads.** Sharing each body's weight among all of its links let a building hang from one corner, frozen in mid-air. Loading only the links to fixed structure fixed that, and the weakest link now gives first.
- **Hinges pivot on an edge.** Anchoring at the edge of the contact, toward the hanging mass and down, stops a swinging deck from grinding into the wall it came from. Shrinking loose chunks' collision hulls by 3% stops broken neighbours from starting out pressed together. Together they took out the energy explosions (chunks flung 20 m up).
- **Region solves make big buildings practical** for local damage (12–15 ms a step). The closing full solve keeps them honest. (Since replaced by exact downdates; see below.)
- **A fallen piece should hang by one contact, not float on ghost springs.** Exact downdates need every piece cut loose to stay positive definite once its last link comes out. The plan was a ghost spring to the ground on every node (1e-9 of the median stiffness), and it caused two problems:
  - It moved a cantilever root's shear by 3e-7 of itself.
  - Each piece that fell drove a pivot down to about √ε, and the rotation there scaled rounding up about 30,000×. The residual climbed from 1e-12 to 8e-9 in 20 steps. A ghost 1,000× stiffer slowed the climb 30× but moved utilization by 4e-3.

  The fix: a free body hanging off a single contact adds no stiffness to what it hangs from. So each fallen piece now keeps exactly one link, or none if it still touches the ground. The standing structure solves exactly, no pivot gets small, and a piece's last link costs no downdate at all. The residual growth left over belonged to the structure: a fresh factor shows the same on a building about to fall. So refinement is triggered by the scale-free backward error, not the residual.
- **A clamp before the step is not a safety net.** Velocities were clamped to 24 m/s before each step, but a step that threw a chunk at 120 m/s still moved it 2 m before the clamp saw it, and a trapped chunk was thrown again on every step. The guard undoes the step for that body, both before the next step and before drawing.
- **Don't trust Rapier 0.20's per-body joint walk.** `forEachJointHandleAttachedToRigidBody` disagreed with a direct count of the joints on 30–100 bodies at a time in a busy scene, which made bodies look roped when they weren't. Joints are now counted from the joints themselves, and the guard no longer asks whether a trapped body has joints: it always has its hinges let go first.
- **Hanging pieces never sleep, and making them sleep was worse.** Pressed against the wall it broke from, a hanging chunk hums at a few cm/s in place: the hinge pulls it in and the contact pushes it out, so Rapier's velocity test never passes. Putting hanging bodies to sleep once they stopped moving brought physics at rest from about 3 ms to 1.2 ms in one probe. But it tripled hinge tears in the capture (285 against 82–85): a body asleep is frozen, and the nets it was part of tore against it. Reverted. Putting free bodies to sleep the same way also let rubble settle into them unanswered (one went through the floor).
- **Test rigs must run the scene's solver settings.** At Rapier's default 4 iterations the test rig's hanging chunk shook several cm a second and never settled. At the scene's 6 it went still, which changed whether it could settle into rubble. The rig now uses 6 and the scene's rule that nothing a hinge holds settles.
- **Count every event, not a sample.** The soak sampled speeds every half second and saw one 88 m/s chunk in one run. Counting every throw found thousands in a bad run, and the details on each (where the body came from, how old it was, its joints) pointed straight at hinge nets on fresh shards. The same details showed bodies with joints reporting no hinges. The cause was a link over the budget being dropped twice, so its bodies' link counts went to zero while their other links still held.
- **Spark's `burstAt` moves the emitter**, so all of a frame's bursts on one emitter spawn at its last position. Effects use small round-robin emitter pools.
- **An independent visual review earned its keep.** A fresh reviewer, given only the frames and the Season 12 list, found:
  - chunks floating in mid-air: rubble that settled on a roof stayed put after the roof fell;
  - loose chunks drawn larger than their collision hulls, so neighbours looked sunk into each other;
  - hinge "rebar" lines that read as rendering artifacts;
  - structures holding rigid and then dropping all at once;
  - a container that never visibly swung;
  - bubbly dust, paper-white smoke in windows, and no small rubble.

  Each one was fixed, and the floating rubble got a regression test.
- **Read input per rendered frame, not per fixed step.** Spark's presses and mouse deltas last one frame, and at 120+ fps half the frames have no fixed step. `pnpm check:input` presses keys two frames apart and checks every press lands.
- **Measure synthesized sounds.** The layered boom and crack summed past full scale (peaks of 1.46 and 1.41) and the creak came out nearly silent (peak 0.14). Every take is now normalized to a 0.85 peak, and loudness is set per sound.
- **Check each sound against the signature of what it's meant to be** (`pnpm check:sounds`):
  - the boom: a fast attack, a long rolling decay, low-heavy, darkening as it fades;
  - the crack: a sharp, bright, short attack;
  - the crumble: many separate impacts;
  - the creak: a pitched, stuttering groan around 60 Hz;
  - the thud: short and dull.

  The check found the boom ending in a rising hiss rather than a rumble: one 12 dB-per-octave filter leaked too much high noise at a low cutoff. It also showed the boom was really a short pop, 45 dB down by 0.6 s. Two low-pass stages in series, a held roar and a low rolling rumble fixed both.
- **Measure the mix, not just the sounds.** Each sound was right on its own, but `pnpm clip` now mixes what the game played from the camera's path (`soundtrack.ts`: the engine's panning, falloff, voice caps and limiter). The first mix peaked at −25.5 dBFS. The engine's point-source falloff is full volume at 1 m, which put a blast 20 m away at −26 dB. Each sound now has a reach that matches its size: 14 m for the boom, 6–7 m for the rest. The mix also showed that a building coming down played one crumble and one thud: only shattering made a sound. Landings are heard now, sized by all the mass that came down in the step. That gives 94 thuds and 22 crumbles over the collapse, and the film peaks at −2.8 dBFS (the limiter holds the rocket).
- **Synthesized sounds did not survive a listen.** Greg heard that clip and called the sound horrible (the picture good). The sounds are now ElevenLabs takes, and the takes needed as much checking as the synthesis did:
  - Prompts miss. The first thuds had under 7% of their energy below 200 Hz (a tick, not a thud), the crumbles were hiss above 6 kHz, and the creaks were sub-bass a laptop can't play. The rewritten thud prompt then went all sub (94% below 80 Hz). So a thud is a sub hit plus a mid knock, a creak is a metal creak over a low groan, and a boom gets a crack on its front and debris behind it. `takes.json` holds the layers.
  - Level by loudness, then limit. Capped at a peak, the sharp sounds (boom, crack, thud) came out 5–12 dB under the rest. A 400 ms loudness window also hears a 50 ms impact as quieter than it sounds; the takes are leveled on 100 ms and limited (up to 8 dB) to −1 dBFS.
  - Start a crack at its snap. The loudest snap in a crack take came up to 134 ms after a faint crackle, late against the glue breaking on screen.
  - Read the film's sound log (`clip-sounds.json`), not just its level. It showed a creak exactly every second for ten seconds (a chunk stays near its limit; the gap is now 2–4.5 s and random), a thud every 70 ms through the collapse (a patter of four takes; the collapse now roars with the mass landed in the last half second), rubble nets snapping every 0.25 s (only tears over 20 kN snap now), a container that never clanged (it lands at 3 m/s, spread over two steps), and dead air between events (the air bed).

## From moving it into the engine

- **A collider's pose is its shape's frame, not the object it stands for.** The sandbox built each standing chunk's hull in world coordinates, so every building collider sat at the origin, and the rubble support check ("does it rest on something lower than itself?") had quietly counted every standing chunk as lower. Moving to shapes built once around each chunk's centroid put the building colliders at their chunks, and the same comparison would have let rubble lying against a wall fall. The check now says what it meant: standing structure holds, anything else holds if it is lower.
- **Build a convex shape once, place it by offset.** A chunk is a convex prism whose faces are known, so its convex mesh (vertices relative to its centroid, full size and 3% shrunk, fan-triangulated faces) is made once at load, and a body places it with a collider offset. The sandbox recomputed a hull on every detach, split and settle. All 1,795 arena chunks build as convex meshes; a hull fallback is there for slivers Rapier refuses, and it has never fired.
- **Compare a port on what is deterministic, and on ranges for the rest.** On a fixed clock the engine's version of the `A-ground` collapse matches the destruction repo's sandbox (on the same solver) through the strain cascade: the same chunks standing, bodies made, hinges formed and torn, and steps at 0.5 s and 1 s. It diverges as soon as debris hits the ground. That divergence is Rapier's, not the port's: switching chunks from convex meshes to hulls of the same points moved the shatter count at 4 s from 256 to 185, and starting the same collapse one frame later moved the sandbox's own from 134 to 245. One run's counts after an impact say nothing; the cascade, and the spread over a few starts, do.
- **Colliders join the query structures on the next physics step.** A raycast straight after building a structure, before any step, hit nothing. Scenes never notice (they step before anything aims); tests have to step once.
- **A change made while a step is in flight is not covered by that step's answer.** Damage or a load change sent while the worker was solving could be dropped when that step came back stable, ending the cascade with the change unsolved. `Destruction` marks such changes and keeps the cascade going (workstream C found it).
- **The engine owns the worker, and the worker's imports are a lint rule.** A game should not repeat the `new Worker(new URL(...))` incantation, the inline host for fixed clocks, or a fallback for a worker that will not load. Keeping three, Rapier and the engine barrel out of the worker is a `no-restricted-imports` rule rather than a habit; the worker chunk is the solver alone, 44.5 KB. A worker that fails replays every request into a main-thread runner, so the cascade continues where it was.
- **A subsystem that takes its renderer, audio and particles as optional runs whole in Node.** `Destruction` without a renderer, a `ParticleSystem` or an `AudioSystem` is silent and invisible but otherwise complete, so `Destruction.test.ts` builds a building, blows a hole in it, follows the cascade and checks that dispose leaves no body, collider or joint, all in vitest.

## From the first playtest

- **Weight resting on the structure is the structure's to carry.** Greg blew out the street-level front of the sandbox's left building and its top stayed in the air. Strain had broken the joints at the tops of the interior columns, under the weight, and cut the top loose. Physically it still sat on those columns, but as debris its weight was nobody's: Strain only ever saw hanging loads, so the columns felt nothing and stood. Standing chunks now report contact forces over a tonne (`forceEvents` on their colliders, `physics.contactForces`), and what loose bodies press on them goes to Strain with the hanging loads. A body that settles there keeps its weight on them while it lies there. A load counts once two reads in a row (six fixed steps apart) see it, so a landing's spike does not break a floor.
- **Debris is not structure, and what tells is that it never moved.** With the weight counted, the columns broke, and their blocks, stacked exactly where the columns had stood, held the top up as before: rigid blocks carry any load. A standing chunk Strain breaks under resting weight over 10 times its own is now crushed (gone, with grit, dust and a crumble). Loose debris took four tries:
  - Pressed by over 80 times its own weight: on the fixed clock the top came down, but in real time (strain in the worker) it stuck in a quarter of the runs, on two dozen panels of the failed storey each pressed by under 60 times theirs. At 40, the falling top's own walls were ground away and left a hollow shell.
  - At rest, over 20 times and 100 kN: every real-time run came down, but the top's walls ground away again while it paused.
  - Only single chunks: worse. The panels holding the top up were whole wall elements, standing upright on the ground where they had stood, and some had settled into rubble in place.
  - Only the bottom of a pile (resting on the ground, standing structure or rubble), rubble included: every run came down, and a third of the building went to grit, mostly in the heap it landed in. A log of every crush showed why: a heap's bottom is pressed as hard as the panels under a stuck top (medians 190–1,000 kN against 360–1,300).

  What does tell them apart is position: the panels under a stuck top are still where they were built, and a heap has fallen metres. The rule now crushes debris within half a metre of where it was built, at the bottom of a pile, at rest, pressed by over 20 times its own weight and 100 kN, on two reads in a row. Eight real-time runs out of eight come down, with 17–38 crushes a run.
- **Rubble settles only on what is settled.** A still body became rubble whatever it lay on, and stillness counted while hinges held it. The top's pieces lay still on the few pieces hinged to the stubs, turned to rubble at full height within a second, and stayed there when what was under them later fell. A body now settles only if one of its chunks rests on the ground, standing structure or rubble (not a loose body, which can still move away), and its still time starts when nothing holds it. A survey of every scenario after ten seconds: chunks that came loose but never moved half a metre went from 756 to 6 for the blown-out front, and fell or held level elsewhere.
- **Rubble has to keep earning its place, and nothing hanging earns it.** Greg's second report was a whole top floor hanging in the sky with nothing under it, its broken pieces dangling below. A probe that fires like a player at both buildings (`captures/midair.mjs`: rockets round the ground storey, then rockets and charges through the engine's raycast) and then joins every chunk's box to what it touches, looking for groups the ground never reaches, left 50 to 160 chunks in the air in every heavy run. Four holes, each closed and checked with the probe:
  - The body budget settled the stillest bodies wherever they were, mid-fall included, and much of a building's top settled at once while it leaned on standing walls that fell a moment later. Over budget, a body now settles only if it lies on something. A quarter over, hinges let go of what they have held longest, so it can fall and settle. Clearing the oldest happens only at half again over: it had taken a hundred bodies away at once, mid-fall.
  - Rubble re-checked its support only when a chunk near it was destroyed. Candidates were gathered from every chunk now and then, and losses expired before a long queue reached them. Rubble is now indexed by a 2 m grid: a loss gathers what lies across from it at once, and again every second while it lasts. A crushed or cleared loose body is a loss too, and so is debris a blast pushes away. With nothing waiting, a slow sweep goes through all of it, for debris that rolled away unseen.
  - The check counted the pieces hanging from the rubble as holding it up: they were below it. Loose bodies hinges hold no longer count.
  - Nor did a chunk asleep and wedged in the rubble around it, which held that rubble up as the rubble held it. Loose debris counts only if it lies on something settled itself.

  Nothing was left in the air after that in 16 heavy runs, or in 8 on the left building alone, and nothing was cleared. The cost is more loose bodies at the peak of a two-building collapse (450–530 against 360–440): its longest frames went from about 32 ms to 40–56, a handful of frames a run. A collapse of one building measured the same as before.
- **Held up means a path to the ground, not something lower touching it.** Greg's third report came from a showcase film: the tower's top hanging in the sky after its last corner was blown out, pieces trickling from under it. Replayed on the fixed clock, 339 chunks of rubble had no path to the ground a second after the corner went, and the shape-query check called 4 to 13 of them unsupported at any moment: each layer was held up by the rubble under it until that fell, so the top peeled away from below for three and a half seconds. Every earlier round had closed one way the check was fooled; this was the check working as designed, and any check that only looks at a chunk's neighbours passes a floating stack everywhere but at its bottom. The probes had missed it because they judged the end state (5 to 25 s on, skipping anything awake), and a float that clears itself in a few seconds is exactly what a player or a camera sees.
  Settling now records what each chunk rests on, both ways (the same shape query), and rubble stays only while a path of those links leads down to the ground, standing structure, or loose debris still in place. Loose debris holds until it moves 10 cm off, so a failed post standing where it stood still holds what lies on it. When a link goes, the rubble above with no other path falls in the same step, as the bodies it settled as. The first version left the top up: a settled floor's chunks lie side by side, and only those over a wall rest on anything lower, so the rest had no links and held up what lay on them. The chunks of one settling were one rigid body, and now hold or fall as one.
  On the same replay, no rubble lost its path from the first quarter second, and the mid-air probe found nothing in the air after 15 s in 4 heavy runs of 4. Against the old check on the same machine, the tower came down faster (median 12.2 ms against 18.2, 26 frames over 33 ms against 48: a settling falling whole is cheaper than one peeling a chunk at a time), barrages measured the same (longest frames 42–55 ms in both), and the budget cleared 9 chunks over four barrages against 234. The shape-query recheck and its sweep stay behind the links, for loose debris that rolls out from under rubble without a loss being seen.
- **On WebGPU a `BatchedMesh` is a draw per instance.** Greg asked why the tower wasn't in the sandbox, and whether it would cost too much. Standing, nearly nothing; but while the left building came down, frames over 33 ms went from 0–2 to 14, with every system's time unchanged. The renderer's count gave it away: 5,400 draw calls a frame for 1,800 chunks, 10,100 with the tower. three's WebGPU backend issues one `drawIndexed` per instance of a `BatchedMesh` (WebGPU has no multi-draw), three passes a frame. The chunks are now one plain mesh whose vertices carry their chunk's id; the vertex stage reads the chunk's matrix and tint from two float textures, and since the shadow passes take the material's `positionNode`, shadows follow. 43 draws a frame with or without the tower, every still pixel-identical to before, and the tower is in the sandbox by default.
- **Rapier's contact forces read high, and asleep they read nothing.** A block at rest read (n + 1) / n of its weight with n solver iterations (twice it at one, 1.25 at four, 1.17 at six): the stabilization pass is summed in. `contactForces` scales it back. A sleeping body's contacts are not solved, so it reports nothing; `Destruction` keeps the last load of a body that falls asleep. Rapier's auto-drain also left force events queued across steps; the engine drops the unread ones before each step, so a read sees one step.
- **A draw of no vertices is a WebGPU warning.** With both of the container's ropes cut, the rope lines drew zero vertices and the soak flagged the console. They hide when there is nothing to draw.
